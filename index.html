What's being simulated
  • Every bee is an individual with its own age, physiological wear, job and foraging memory; every cell (~140,000 in two deep boxes) holds its own egg, larva, pupa, nectar, honey or bee-bread. The comb view shows a live sample of the bees on the selected frame.
  • Brood development: worker 3 d egg → larva capped day 9 → emerges day 21; drone 3 / 10 / 24 d; queen 3 / 8 / 16 d. Larvae are fed by nurse bees (≈3 larvae per nurse) using pollen and honey; underfed larvae are removed.
  • Age polyethism: cell cleaning → nursing → food storing, comb building & capping → guarding/fanning → foraging at ~3 weeks. Forager age shifts with colony need (social inhibition by older foragers).
  • Lifespan follows workload: summer bees wear out in ~5–6 weeks (foragers die after ~800 km of flight or from predators), while autumn-reared "winter bees" with little brood to feed live 4–6 months.
  • Foraging: foragers fly at ~25 km/h, carry ~40 mg nectar or ~16 mg pollen, and burn sugar in flight. Profitable patches are advertised by waggle dances — the angle from vertical equals the angle from the sun's azimuth, and the waggle run lengthens with distance; nearby sources get short round dances. Recruits follow dances; scouts discover new flowers; poor patches are abandoned. Pollen foraging tracks brood demand.
  • Honey making: nectar (30–45 % sugar) is handed to receiver bees, stored, and fanned down to ~18 % water, then capped with wax. Long unloading queues raise the dance threshold (tremble-dance effect).
  • Seasons & weather (~45°N): real sun position and day length, daily temperature and rain. No flight below ~12 °C, at night or in rain. Bees cluster below ~14 °C; brood that too few bees can cover is chilled. Brood rearing stops in late autumn, drones are evicted, and the colony overwinters on stored honey.
  • Comb pattern emerges from simple rules (Camazine 1991; Montovan, Karst, Jones & Seeley 2013; Johnson 2009): the queen walks the comb, biased toward the warm centre, and lays only in empty cells near brood; receivers put nectar in random cells but favour high ones (honey above brood); pollen is packed close to the brood; stores next to brood are eaten first; nectar dropped in the brood nest is moved out. No layout is pre-programmed — brood nest, pollen band and honey arch form and shift by themselves.
  • Queen biology: laying up to ~1,800 eggs/day by season, pollen and nurse supply; drone eggs in drone comb in spring/summer. Crowding triggers swarming: queen cups along the bottom of brood combs, the old queen leaves with about half the bees once the first cells are capped.
  • Rival queens: the first virgin to emerge hunts the others — she chews a hole in the side of each queen cell (most mature first) and stings the queen inside; workers tear the cells down. Two free virgins fight to the death. In strong colonies workers instead guard the cells, the free virgin "toots" and confined queens "quack", and she leaves with an afterswarm before the next queen is released. In supersedure, mother and daughter may coexist until the daughter has mated.
  • House-hunting (Seeley): ~5 % of a swarm scout for cavities; they dance more for better sites (≈40 L, small entrance, high up, south-facing); each scout's dancing fades ~15 circuits per trip; when ~15–30 scouts are at one site at once (quorum) they pipe and the swarm flies there — usually after one to three days, and usually to the best site found.
  • Winter: autumn bees that have little brood to rear keep a nurse-like physiology and live for months; they stay house bees until brood rearing resumes. The queen restarts laying after the winter solstice once it is mild enough — the warmth needed falls as the days lengthen (Nürnberger et al. 2018). The cluster burns roughly 20 kg of honey by spring.
  • Entrance: every flight starts and ends at the landing board, so the entrance view draws landings and take-offs at the colony's actual rate (× the simulation speed), along with guards, fanners on hot days and evenings of nectar ripening, orientation flights of young bees, and bearding on hot evenings.
  • Not modelled: varroa & disease, robbing, competition with other colonies, propolis.
